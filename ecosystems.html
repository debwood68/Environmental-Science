Section 1
What is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms (biotic components) interacting with their physical environment (abiotic components) as a system. Ecosystems can be as vast as the open ocean or as small as a rock pool — the scale varies, but the principles are the same.

Ecosystem A dynamic complex of plant, animal, and microorganism communities and the non-living environment, all interacting as a functional unit.

The biotic components include all living organisms — producers, consumers, and decomposers. The abiotic components include sunlight, temperature, water, soil chemistry, and atmospheric gases. Neither can function without the other.

⚡ Biotic Components

Producers — photosynthetic organisms (plants, algae, cyanobacteria) that convert solar energy into chemical energy.

Consumers — organisms that obtain energy by eating other organisms. Primary consumers eat producers; secondary and tertiary consumers eat other animals.

Decomposers — bacteria and fungi that break down dead organic matter, returning nutrients to the soil and atmosphere.

🌡️ Abiotic Components

Climate — temperature and rainfall patterns determine which species can survive in an area.

Soil — nutrient availability, pH, and drainage affect plant communities and therefore everything above them in the food web.

Light — drives photosynthesis. Depth in aquatic systems determines which producers can survive.

Water availability — a major limiting factor for terrestrial ecosystems.

Section 2
Energy Flow Through Ecosystems

Energy enters ecosystems almost entirely through photosynthesis, where producers convert solar radiation into chemical energy stored in organic molecules. This energy is then transferred through the ecosystem as organisms consume one another — but critically, energy is lost at every transfer.

The 10% rule states that only approximately 10% of the energy stored in one trophic level is available to the next. The remaining ~90% is lost as heat through metabolic processes (respiration), movement, and waste. This is why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 trophic levels — there simply isn't enough energy to support further levels.

From Food Chains to Food Webs

A food chain shows a single linear pathway of energy transfer. In reality, most organisms feed on multiple species and are eaten by multiple predators — creating a complex food web. Food webs are more realistic and show the true interconnectedness of ecosystems.

Section 3
Nutrient Cycling

Unlike energy — which flows through ecosystems in one direction and is eventually lost as heat — nutrients cycle continuously between living organisms and the physical environment. The most important nutrient cycles in ecosystems are the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle, and the water cycle.

Nutrient Cycling The movement and exchange of organic and inorganic matter back into the production of living matter. Decomposers play a critical role by breaking down dead organic material and returning nutrients to the soil.

🍂 The Role of Decomposers

Without decomposers, nutrients would remain locked in dead organic matter and ecosystems would eventually run out of available nutrients. Bacteria and fungi secrete enzymes that break down complex organic molecules into simpler inorganic forms — releasing nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon back into the soil, water, and atmosphere.

The rate of decomposition is affected by temperature, moisture, and oxygen availability. This is why tropical rainforests cycle nutrients extremely rapidly (warm, wet conditions) while boreal forests and peatlands cycle them very slowly (cold, waterlogged, low oxygen).

🌱 The Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is essential for building proteins and DNA. Although the atmosphere is ~78% nitrogen gas (N₂), most organisms cannot use it directly. Nitrogen fixation by specialised bacteria (e.g. Rhizobium in legume root nodules) converts N₂ into ammonium (NH₄⁺), which plants can absorb.

Nitrification converts ammonium to nitrate (NO₃⁻). Denitrification by anaerobic bacteria returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere, completing the cycle. Human activities (fertilisers, combustion) have significantly disrupted the nitrogen cycle.

Key Distinction: Energy flows through ecosystems in one direction — it enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. Nutrients cycle within ecosystems — the same atoms are used over and over again by successive generations of organisms. The carbon atoms in your body may once have been part of a dinosaur, a rainforest tree, or an ancient ocean.

Section 4
Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the process by which the structure of a biological community evolves over time following a disturbance or on a newly available substrate. Communities change in a broadly predictable sequence, with each stage (called a seral stage) modifying the environment in ways that make it more suitable for the next community to establish.

Succession ends when the community reaches a relatively stable state called the climax community — the endpoint that a particular climate and geography tends to produce. In Scotland, the climax community for much of the country would be native Caledonian forest, though human activity has prevented most land from reaching this state.

Primary Succession

Occurs on bare, lifeless substrate where no soil exists — for example, after a lava flow, a retreating glacier, or on newly deposited sand dunes. This is a slow process that can take hundreds to thousands of years.

Secondary Succession

Occurs where a community has been disturbed or removed but soil remains — for example, after a forest fire, agricultural abandonment, or flooding. Secondary succession is much faster than primary succession because soil and seed banks already exist.

An abandoned Scottish farm field may progress through grassland → scrub → birch woodland → mature mixed woodland within 100–200 years.

Why Does Succession Happen?

Each seral stage changes the abiotic environment in ways that benefit later species but disadvantage current ones. Pioneer plants produce organic matter that builds soil. Trees create shade that suppresses light-demanding species. Each community effectively prepares the ground for the next.

This process of community-driven environmental change is called facilitation — the dominant mechanism in most successional sequences.

Section 5
Biodiversity & Ecosystem Stability

Biodiversity — the variety of species, genes, and habitats within an ecosystem — is not just important for ethical or aesthetic reasons. It has profound functional consequences for how ecosystems work and how resilient they are to disturbance.

Ecosystem Resilience The capacity of an ecosystem to absorb disturbance and reorganise while undergoing change, so as to retain essentially the same structure, function, and identity.

High-biodiversity ecosystems tend to be more stable and resilient because they have more redundancy — if one species is lost, another can often fulfil a similar ecological role. Low-biodiversity ecosystems (like monoculture farmland) are highly vulnerable to disease, pests, and climate variability.

🔬 Case Study — The Yellowstone Wolves: When grey wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 after a 70-year absence, the effects cascaded through the entire ecosystem. Elk avoided grazing in valleys and riversides, allowing vegetation to recover. This stabilised riverbanks, changed river courses, and increased biodiversity across the park — a dramatic demonstration of how a single species can control an entire ecosystem. This phenomenon is called a trophic cascade.
